What comes up in the room

I don't think the tool is the problem. I think it's the blank box.

I run a mastermind for founders, and this surfaces in nearly every room. Someone has built a real company, they carry a team and a payroll and a calendar that mostly belongs to other people, and they've quietly decided this whole thing is overhyped. When I ask what they tried, it was a question they could have googled.

None of them had a wrong idea about what it does. They had no idea where it fit a week like theirs, and a list of use cases was never going to tell them, because their week isn't on anybody's list.

So this starts from the other end, with the last seven days of your own calendar.

The hour

Eight phases, one sitting.

You answer questions, it does the work, and by the end there are things running that weren't running before.

The usual first hour

"What can you help me with?" A list of twelve generic capabilities. You pick one, try it, it's fine, you close the tab.

This one

"What did you do more than twice last week? What did you do at 9pm that you resented?" Then a ranked table of where it genuinely helps, and the honest list of where it won't.

One is a brochure. The other is a diagnosis.

  1. 1

    The audit

    Eight questions about your last seven days. Then a scored table: hours it costs you, how much you hate it, how well this actually works. Including a straight answer on what to skip.

  2. 2

    A memory

    The step nearly everyone misses. A context file holding who you are, what you're building, and the people you'll mention constantly, so you stop reintroducing yourself every conversation. Everything else gets better once this exists.

  3. 3

    What never gets pasted

    Client information, anything you're under obligation to protect, anything that would hurt if it surfaced. Three rules you can remember, plus the habit of checking anything you'd act on.

  4. 4

    One real win

    The top item from your own table, done for real with your own material. Not a demo. You need to feel it once.

  5. 5

    How to make it better

    You edit what it made. It reads your edits as rules and writes them down. This is the habit that separates people who get value from people who give up, and almost nobody stumbles into it.

  6. 6

    Goals, and something that holds you to them

    Three goals, what done looks like, and a weekly check-in that reads your calendar before it asks how the week went.

  7. 7

    The part of life that isn't work

    Travel, money, the kids' logistics, the research you've been meaning to do for months. One of them set up before you finish.

  8. 8

    What comes next

    Where to go after this, in order, based on what it learned about you. Including which things aren't for you.

The accountability piece

Your calendar doesn't lie.

Most goal trackers ask how the week went. You tell them what you wish were true, they record it, and everyone feels fine.

This one looks first. It reads where your hours actually went, works out which of your three goals got any of them, and then asks about the one that didn't. By name, specifically, and only one question.

When something has sat untouched for three weeks, it says so rather than letting it drift. And it holds open the possibility that you set the wrong goal in the first place. Dropping something on purpose gets recorded as a decision, not a failure, which is the part most systems get backwards.

  • Your goals live in a document you own. The dashboard is just the view of it.
  • The check-in runs whether you open the page or not, which is the whole point.
  • Two questions maximum. Nobody has ever been coached into action by a wall of prompts.
  • If the week was consumed by something that mattered more, it says that and lets the goals wait.
